Animal Care

Caring for reptiles means understanding the specific needs of the animal in your care. While many reptiles share basic needs such as appropriate temperatures, clean water, proper nutrition, and safe housing, every species has its own requirements.

ScaleWise Education provides general reptile-care education and species-specific resources for the animals featured in our programs. Our goal is to help owners understand their animals and provide responsible, informed care.

Important: Care requirements can vary based on species, age, individual health, and environment. These resources are intended for education and should not replace advice from a qualified reptile veterinarian.

Basic Reptile Care

Although every species is different, responsible reptile care generally begins with the same fundamentals.

Enclosure

An enclosure should provide enough space for the animal to move naturally and should be secure, escape-proof, and appropriate for the species. It should also allow the animal to access the environmental conditions it needs.

Enclosure size should be based on the animal's expected adult size, activity level, natural behavior, and habitat requirements rather than relying solely on gallons or enclosure volume.

Whenever practical, enclosures should be planned with the animal's expected adult size in mind.

Temperature

Reptiles rely on their environment to regulate their body temperature. A proper enclosure should provide an appropriate temperature gradient, allowing the animal to move between warmer and cooler areas as needed.

Temperatures should be measured with reliable thermometers rather than estimated by touch.

Heat-producing equipment should generally be controlled with an appropriate thermostat.

Species-specific temperature requirements should always take priority over general recommendations.

Humidity & Hydration

Many reptiles require specific humidity levels to remain healthy and properly shed their skin. Fresh, clean water should be available when appropriate for the species, and humidity should be monitored with a reliable hygrometer.

Humidity requirements vary significantly between species. Higher humidity is not automatically better, and humidity should always be considered together with appropriate ventilation.

Lighting

Some reptiles require specific lighting, including UVB lighting, while others have different requirements. Lighting should be selected based on the species rather than using a one-size-fits-all approach.

A heat-producing bulb and a UVB source serve different purposes. Producing visible light or heat does not automatically mean that a bulb provides appropriate UVB.

Feeding

A reptile's diet should match its species, age, size, activity level, and body condition. Prey items or other foods should be appropriately sized and safely sourced.

Owners should also monitor feeding habits, growth, weight, and body condition rather than relying solely on a feeding schedule.

Cleaning

Enclosures should be kept clean and sanitary. Waste should be removed regularly, water dishes should be cleaned, and the enclosure should receive appropriate deeper cleaning when necessary.

Cleaning products must be safe for reptiles and thoroughly removed before the animal is returned to the enclosure.

Handling

Reptiles should be handled calmly and respectfully. Support the animal's body properly, avoid unnecessary restraint, and learn to recognize signs of stress or defensive behavior.

Not every reptile enjoys frequent handling, and responsible care means respecting the animal's limits.

Health Monitoring

Regularly observe your reptile for changes in:

  • Appetite
  • Weight and body condition
  • Activity
  • Breathing
  • Skin and shedding
  • Eyes, mouth, and nose
  • Droppings and urates
  • Behavior

Knowing what is normal for your animal makes it easier to recognize when something may be wrong.

When to Contact a Veterinarian

Seek veterinary care when your reptile has concerning symptoms such as difficulty breathing, serious injury, unexplained weight loss, persistent refusal to eat, abnormal swelling, severe lethargy, repeated problems shedding, or other significant changes in health or behavior.

When possible, establish a relationship with a veterinarian experienced in reptile medicine before an emergency occurs.

General Rules of Thumb

The following guidelines provide general starting points for responsible reptile care. They are intended to help owners understand the relationship between an animal's size, behavior, and environmental needs.

These are not universal minimum requirements. Requirements can vary significantly between species, and species-specific care recommendations should always take priority.

Enclosure Size

A properly sized enclosure should provide enough usable space for the animal to move, thermoregulate, rest, hide, explore, and display appropriate natural behaviors.

When determining enclosure size, consider:

Expected Adult Size + Activity Level + Natural Habitat + Behavior + Environmental Requirements

Gallons alone should not be used to determine whether an enclosure is appropriate. Two enclosures with the same volume can have very different dimensions and therefore provide very different amounts of usable floor or climbing space.

Snakes

For many terrestrial snakes, a useful general starting point is:

  • Enclosure length should be at least as long as the snake's expected adult body length.
  • Enclosure width should generally provide approximately half the snake's body length or more.
  • Enclosure height should provide enough space for the species' normal behavior, including climbing when appropriate.
  • More active species may require substantially more space.
  • Arboreal species require greater vertical space and appropriate climbing opportunities.

For example, a terrestrial snake expected to reach approximately 4 feet in length may have a starting enclosure around 4 feet long by 2 feet wide, with height determined by the species and its behavior.

These measurements are starting points rather than universal minimums. Species-specific recommendations should always be followed when they call for additional space.

Terrestrial Lizards

For terrestrial lizards, usable floor space is generally more important than total enclosure volume.

As a general rule:

  • Longer-bodied lizards require longer enclosures.
  • Active species require more floor space than sedentary species of the same size.
  • The enclosure should allow the animal to move freely between warm and cool areas.
  • Adequate space should remain available after hides, climbing structures, water dishes, and other furnishings are added.
  • Larger enclosures are generally preferable when appropriate environmental conditions can still be maintained.

A lizard should not be considered appropriately housed simply because it physically fits inside an enclosure.

Arboreal Lizards

For arboreal species, enclosure height and usable vertical space become especially important.

The enclosure should provide enough room for the animal to:

  • Climb
  • Perch
  • Hide
  • Move between different environmental zones
  • Display normal species-specific behaviors

Branches, vines, plants, and other structures should create usable climbing pathways rather than simply filling empty space.

An enclosure with adequate floor area may still be inappropriate for a strongly arboreal species if it does not provide sufficient vertical space.

Tortoises

Tortoises require more space than their slow movement may suggest.

For tortoises:

  • Prioritize floor area rather than enclosure height.
  • Provide enough space to move between warm and cool areas.
  • Provide appropriate hides and environmental enrichment.
  • Allow adequate room for walking, exploring, digging, and other natural behaviors when appropriate.
  • Outdoor housing can provide substantially more usable space when climate, security, and species requirements allow.

Tortoise housing should be considered in terms of usable floor area rather than gallons.

Aquatic Turtles

Aquatic turtles require enough water volume and swimming space for their size and behavior.

Consider:

  • Adult shell length
  • Swimming space
  • Water depth
  • Water quality
  • Filtration capacity
  • Basking space
  • Access to a completely dry basking area
  • Expected adult size

Aquatic turtle housing should not be determined solely by the number of gallons. The dimensions and shape of the enclosure also determine how much usable swimming space the animal has.

Temperature

A reptile enclosure should generally provide a temperature gradient rather than maintaining the entire enclosure at one temperature.

A properly designed enclosure should allow the animal to move between warmer and cooler areas as needed.

General principles include:

  • Place the primary heat source toward one end of the enclosure when appropriate.
  • Use a thermostat to control heat-producing equipment.
  • Measure temperatures with reliable thermometers.
  • Measure temperatures where the animal actually spends time.
  • Follow species-specific temperature recommendations.

The goal is not simply to create a "hot side" and a "cold side," but to give the animal meaningful control over its body temperature.

Humidity & Hydration

Humidity should be based on the animal's natural biology rather than using a single humidity target for all reptiles.

Consider:

  • Ambient humidity
  • Humid hides
  • Substrate moisture
  • Water dishes
  • Misting
  • Ventilation
  • Daily and seasonal changes in humidity

Higher humidity is not automatically better. Proper ventilation and species-appropriate humidity must be considered together.

Fresh, clean water should be provided when appropriate for the species.

Lighting & UVB

Lighting should be selected according to the species rather than simply purchasing a bulb marketed for reptiles.

Some reptiles require significant UVB exposure, while others have different lighting requirements.

Consider:

  • Whether the species naturally receives UVB
  • UVB strength and output
  • Distance between the animal and the UVB source
  • Barriers such as mesh or glass
  • Photoperiod
  • Access to shade
  • Replacement of aging UVB bulbs according to manufacturer recommendations

A heat bulb and a UVB bulb serve different purposes. A heat source does not automatically provide appropriate UVB.

Feeding

Feeding should be based on the animal's species, age, size, activity level, growth, and body condition.

General principles include:

  • Food should be appropriately sized.
  • Prey should be safely sourced when applicable.
  • Feeding frequency should change as the animal grows.
  • Body condition should be monitored regularly.
  • Appetite should be considered alongside other signs of health.
  • A feeding schedule should be treated as a guideline rather than a substitute for observing the animal.

Two animals of the same species and age may not always require identical feeding schedules.

Hides & Enrichment

An enclosure can be large enough while still being a poor habitat.

Animals should have opportunities to perform appropriate natural behaviors. Depending on the species, this may include:

  • Hiding
  • Climbing
  • Digging
  • Exploring
  • Burrowing
  • Basking
  • Foraging
  • Swimming
  • Choosing between different environmental conditions

The goal should be usable space rather than empty space.

A large, empty enclosure does not necessarily provide a better environment than a properly furnished enclosure of the same size.

Growing Animals

Young reptiles often require different amounts of space, food, and environmental structure than adults.

When planning an enclosure, consider how large the animal is expected to become.

Whenever practical, providing an appropriately sized adult enclosure can prevent unnecessary upgrades as the animal grows. However, the enclosure must still be properly furnished and capable of maintaining the environmental conditions required by the animal.

Some species may benefit from smaller or more heavily furnished enclosures during early life stages, particularly when security, feeding, or environmental control would otherwise be difficult.

Remember: Education Comes First

A care guide is a starting point, not a substitute for professional veterinary care. Reptiles can hide signs of illness, so learning your animal's normal behavior and appearance is an important part of responsible ownership.

General rules of thumb can help owners understand the fundamentals, but species-specific research should always be used when making care decisions.

When in doubt, research the specific species or seek advice from a qualified reptile veterinarian.
