Conservation

Conservation is about more than protecting animals. It is about protecting the habitats, ecosystems, and natural processes that allow wildlife to survive. Arizona is home to an incredible variety of reptiles and other wildlife, and many species depend on healthy deserts, grasslands, forests, rivers, and wetlands.

At ScaleWise Education, we believe education is one of the most powerful conservation tools available. When people understand the animals living around them, they are more likely to respect them, protect their habitat, and make responsible decisions when wildlife and people cross paths.

Why Reptiles Matter

Reptiles are an important part of Arizona's ecosystems. Snakes, lizards, turtles, and other reptiles interact with many other species as predators, prey, and competitors.

Predatory reptiles help regulate populations of insects, rodents, and other animals. At the same time, reptiles provide food for birds, mammals, and other predators. This makes them an important part of the food web.

Reptiles also tell us a great deal about the health of their environment. Changes in their populations can provide valuable information about habitat conditions, water availability, pollution, disease, and other environmental pressures.

Arizona is home to more than 100 native reptile species, making our state's herpetofauna an important part of its natural heritage.

Native Species Conservation

Arizona's native reptiles face different challenges depending on their species and habitat. Some are widespread and adaptable, while others have much smaller ranges or depend on specialized habitats.

The Arizona Game & Fish Department uses scientific data to identify species that may need additional conservation attention. Its Arizona Wildlife Conservation Strategy includes a list of Species of Greatest Conservation Need and identifies priorities for protecting vulnerable wildlife and habitats.

Conservation can include protecting habitat, restoring damaged ecosystems, monitoring wildlife populations, improving habitat connectivity, and working with landowners, researchers, agencies, tribes, and conservation organizations.

For example, Arizona conservation partners continue to restore wetlands and other habitats that support imperiled amphibians and reptiles. These projects show how habitat restoration can directly benefit native wildlife.

Habitat Preservation

Wildlife needs more than a place to live. It needs access to food, water, shelter, space, and areas where it can move safely.

Habitat loss and fragmentation can make it harder for animals to find food, water, mates, and suitable shelter. Roads, development, fences, and other human structures can divide habitats and interfere with natural wildlife movement.

Protecting existing habitat is one of the most effective ways to help wildlife. Where habitat has already been damaged, restoration projects can help return important resources to the ecosystem.

Even small choices can make a difference. Staying on designated trails, avoiding unnecessary disturbance of wildlife, protecting native vegetation, and keeping natural areas free of trash can all help reduce human impacts.

Responsible Human/Wildlife Interaction

Seeing a wild reptile can be an exciting experience. The best thing we can give wildlife is usually space.

When encountering a wild reptile:

  • Observe from a safe distance.
  • Do not chase, corner, or harass the animal.
  • Never attempt to handle an unfamiliar wild snake.
  • Keep dogs and other pets away from wildlife.
  • Do not remove wild animals from their habitat simply because they are interesting.
  • Avoid disturbing nests, dens, eggs, or shelter sites.
  • Never feed wild reptiles.
  • If an animal appears injured, contact an appropriate wildlife professional rather than attempting to treat it yourself.
  • Learn to identify local species before assuming an animal is dangerous.

Wildlife encounters do not always require intervention. In many cases, the safest and most conservation-friendly response is simply to give the animal room to continue what it was doing.

Arizona Game & Fish provides resources for understanding and managing human-wildlife interactions while protecting both people and wildlife.

How People Can Help

Conservation does not require everyone to become a biologist. Small actions from many people can have a meaningful impact.

You can help by:

  • Learning to identify native reptiles and other wildlife.
  • Teaching children to respect wild animals rather than fear them.
  • Keeping natural areas clean and free of trash.
  • Staying on designated roads and trails.
  • Keeping pets under control around wildlife.
  • Planting and protecting native vegetation where appropriate.
  • Avoiding unnecessary disturbance of wildlife and habitat.
  • Supporting responsible wildlife rehabilitation and conservation organizations.
  • Volunteering for habitat restoration or wildlife projects.
  • Following Arizona's wildlife laws and regulations.
  • Reporting concerning wildlife activity to the appropriate agency.
  • Sharing accurate information instead of wildlife myths and fear-based stories.

Arizona Game & Fish works with conservation organizations, landowners, volunteers, and other partners on hundreds of wildlife conservation projects throughout the state.
